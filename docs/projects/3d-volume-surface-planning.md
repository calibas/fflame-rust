The framing to start with: a flame attractor generally isn't a surface. It's a measure — often fractal dust or a filament tangle with non-integer dimension — so any technique that assumes a 2-manifold (all meshing, Poisson included) is fighting the data. That splits your options into two families: treat it as a *volume* (faithful) or force it into a *surface* (lossy but gets you conventional lighting for free).

**Volume family**

*Voxelized density + volume rendering.* Splat the chaos game into a 3D histogram instead of 2D — your existing accumulation logic generalizes directly, log-density and all. Render by raymarching with emission/absorption, which preserves exactly the translucent, self-overlapping character flames have. Lighting comes from the density gradient: ∇ρ (central differences on the grid) gives you a normal wherever density is locally surface-like, and you shade with it; where the gradient is weak (dust, filaments) you fall back to pure emissive, which is visually correct there anyway. Shadows are secondary rays through the same density field, or precomputed into a light-space "shadow volume" pass so each frame is one lookup per sample. Ambient occlusion is nearly free: sample density in a small neighborhood sphere. This is the quality ceiling of the realistic options, and it slots into your linear-space pre-tonemap pipeline cleanly.

*Screen-space / depth-buffer shading.* Keep splatting points, but also accumulate nearest depth per pixel. Reconstruct normals from depth derivatives in a post pass, then do deferred lighting plus shadow mapping from the light's view. This is essentially what JWildfire's "solid rendering" mode does. It's by far the easiest to add — no new spatial structure, just extra render targets and two post passes — and it turns the transparent ghost into an opaque lit object. The cost: you've discarded interior structure entirely (only the front surface exists), and depth-derived normals are noisy exactly where the chaos game is sparse, so it composes naturally with your à-trous denoising.

**Surface family**

*Marching cubes on the voxel grid* — not Poisson — is the sane meshing route for you. Poisson reconstruction answers a different problem: given unstructured scanned points *with oriented normals*, find the implicit surface. You don't have normals, you'd have to estimate them (PCA on neighborhoods, unreliable on filaments), and Poisson's global smoothness prior will actively erase fractal detail and inflate dust into blobs. But if you've already voxelized, you have a scalar field, and an isosurface at a density threshold via marching cubes is direct, local, and respects the data. You still lose everything below the threshold and below voxel pitch, but you gain the whole triangle-mesh ecosystem: rasterized shadows, PBR, export to Blender.

*AI point-to-mesh* — skip it. Current models are trained on scans of ordinary objects, choke well below your point counts, and will hallucinate smooth geometry where the attractor has none. Wrong tool categorically.

**One you missed: 3D Gaussian splatting.** Represent samples as anisotropic Gaussians (covariance from local point distribution) and rasterize with EWA-style blending. It handles the dust/filament regime more gracefully than voxels at equal memory and it's very current. But 3DGS as practiced bakes appearance; you'd still need density-gradient normals for relighting, so it ends up being a fancier splatting backend for option 1's shading, not an independent answer.

**Recommendation.** Easiest with real payoff: depth-buffer solid rendering — probably a week of work on your existing pipeline, and it immediately fixes "transparent ghost, can't see surface detail." Best quality and the one that's actually *right* for the medium: voxelized density with gradient shading and shadow rays, since it keeps the volumetric character instead of pretending the attractor is opaque. They're not mutually exclusive — the voxel grid also hands you marching cubes later if you want mesh export. I'd build the depth-buffer mode first as the quick win, with the voxel volume as the destination architecture.

### Another way it's done, but probably not a good fit for us:

"What's mandelbulber do for 3D IFS?"

Completely different paradigm from yours: Mandelbulber never runs the chaos game at all. Everything — Mandelbulbs, Mandelboxes, and its "IFS" fractals — goes through distance-estimation raymarching against an implicit function.

The trick for IFS specifically is the *kaleidoscopic IFS* formulation (from the Fractal Forums community, ~2010, Knighty et al.). Instead of iterating an IFS forward from a point and splatting, you invert the perspective: for a query point in space, iterate the *inverse* system as an escape-time process. Each iteration applies folding operations — plane reflections that map all of space into one fundamental domain (this is what replaces "choose one of N maps": the folds conditionally reflect, which is equivalent to selecting the branch of the inverse IFS that the point belongs to) — followed by a scaling about a fixed point, s·x + offset with s > 1, which is the inverse of the contractive map. Points on the attractor never escape; everything else diverges. Alongside the position you carry a running derivative dr — each iteration multiplies it by the local scale factor — and the distance estimate at bailout is roughly r/|dr| (log-corrected for polynomial formulas like the bulb). That DE drives sphere-tracing: march each ray by the safe distance until you hit the surface.

This is why Mandelbulber gets crisp lighting so trivially. Once you have a DE field, normals are the gradient of DE via central differences (six extra evaluations), shadows are one secondary DE march toward the light, and AO comes almost free from how quickly DE shrinks as you step away from the surface along the normal — the marching structure itself is the illumination machinery. It's the same reason the escape-buffer paper's exterior classification felt lighting-adjacent: a smooth scalar field around the set is most of the battle.

The catch for your renderer is that this only covers self-similar-by-symmetry systems. Kaleidoscopic IFS requires the maps to be expressible as fold + uniform scale — reflections and rotations arranged so one folding sequence stands in for the full inverse branch tree. A general affine IFS with arbitrary per-map contraction ratios, shears, and no shared symmetry doesn't reduce to a fold sequence; there you'd need the true inverse-IFS escape-time (per-point branch search over N inverse maps, i.e., Definition 4.2 from the Hepting–Hart paper, with tree pruning), which is exponentially worse per sample than a fold. And the moment nonlinear flame variations enter, there's no usable inverse or DE at all — same wall the escape buffer hit.

So the honest mapping to your options list: Mandelbulber's approach is a fourth family — implicit/DE raymarching — that's the gold standard for lighting quality *when the system admits a DE*. Your quaternion Julias qualify (the classic |q|·log|q|/(2|dq|) estimator). Purely affine IFS marginally qualify via inverse iteration if N is small and the system is well-behaved. General flames don't, which is why the voxel-density route remains the right destination for you: it's DE raymarching's moral equivalent, with ∇ρ standing in for ∇DE, on data that has no closed-form implicit function.
